Hallstatt Salt Mine: what €49 actually buys, after the 2026 rebuild

Two things to settle before anything else. The €49 everyone quotes is the combined ticket — mine tour plus the funicular up and down. The mine tour on its own is €33. And the mine reopened on 29 August 2026 after a year of works, so most of what you will read about it online describes a funicular that no longer exists.

Our verdict, up front: yes, with one condition. If you have three clear hours and the Bronze Age interests you at all, we think it is the best paid thing in the village. If you have four hours in Hallstatt in total, it will eat the day and you will have seen the inside of a mountain instead of the place you came for.

One honest note on what we sell, since it sits next to this decision. Our seventeen-stop audio walk covers the village — the pier, the lanes, the churchyard, the viewpoint — and it does not go into the mine. The mine has its own guide, included in the ticket. The two fit together rather than compete: the mine is a three-hour block, the walk about ninety minutes, and together they are a full and well-shaped day. If you only have time for one and you want to understand the place rather than tick it off, start in the village.

What €49 buys, and who should not buy it

The price ladder, from Salzwelten's own page. The combined ticket is €49 for an adult and €23 for a child aged 4 to 15. The mine tour without the funicular is €33 and €16. The funicular on its own, for people who want the Skywalk and the view but not the mountain's insides, is €29 and €14. Groups of twenty or more pay €47 a head. There is no family ticket and no online discount: the operator states that concessions are given only in person at the ticket window.

Four kinds of visitor should think twice, and we would rather say so now than at the end.

Anyone on a short day trip. Three hours is the honest figure for the mine alone, and Hallstatt village is a ninety-minute walk at its slowest. If your coach or train gives you four or five hours here, you are choosing between them, not fitting both in.

Anyone also doing the Dachstein cable cars. Both are half-day mountain commitments on opposite sides of the lake. Treating them as two days is the difference between seeing them and surviving them.

Anyone claustrophobic. We will pass this on as the operator phrases it rather than soften it: Salzwelten "strongly suggests not visiting the salt mines" if you are. An operator advising against its own product is rare enough to take seriously.

Wheelchair users. The new funicular and the bistro at the top are step-free and marketed as such; the mine is not. The operator is explicit that conditions inside make a wheelchair visit impossible. When you read "barrier-free" about this attraction, it refers to getting up the mountain.

What is included: the funicular, the tour, the slide, the staircase

Ninety minutes underground, about two kilometres of walking, and you come out of the mountain on the mine train. In between: the miners' slide, which the operator gives as 64 metres and which is optional — stairs with a handrail run alongside it, worth knowing if you have been quietly dreading it. An underground salt lake. A reconstructed grave and a stretch called the Path through Time. And the Bronze Age Cinema, which is the real reason to come and has its own section below.

The practicalities the operator publishes and most write-ups skip. The temperature inside is about 8 °C year round on the operator's FAQ figure, so warm clothes and proper shoes. Protective overalls are compulsory — the operator's word is Ausnahmslos, without exception — and are handed out on site. Photography is fine. Children can go in from age 4, a legal limit under the Austrian mining act rather than a house rule. Dogs cannot, though there are kennels. Small bags can be left free at the Knappenhaus; the larger lockers cost €2 an hour, which over a three-hour visit is not nothing.

How a visit goes, and how long it really takes

Start with the number almost nobody prints, because it is the one that makes people late: Salzwelten asks you to be at the valley station 45 minutes before your tour begins. Not at the mine, at the bottom of the funicular. Everything else follows from that.

How a visit is shaped

  1. Valley station
  2. Funicular, 2.6 min
  3. Walk across the high valley, ~15 min
  4. Guided tour, ~90 min
  5. Mine train out, funicular down

Stages, not proportions. The operator's own duration figures are measured across this whole sequence, from the valley station and back to it.

Now the duration, and here the operator contradicts itself in a way worth untangling, because getting it wrong in the other direction is just as easy. Salzwelten's FAQ says to plan at least two and a half hours, and spells out what that covers: the funicular up and down, plus the short walk across the Hallstatt high valley to the entrance building. Salzwelten's "Discover and experience" page says at least three hours — "for your entire visit" — and sets the meeting point at the valley station.

So both figures are already all-inclusive, measured valley station to valley station. Adding the approach walk on top of them, as a good deal of travel writing does, counts it twice. What the operator does not do is reconcile its own two numbers, and since neither page is more authoritative than the other, the sensible planning figure is the larger one: three hours from arriving at the funicular to coming back down.

The walk itself is the operator's own fifteen minutes, uphill, from the Skywalk at the top of the panorama lift to the Knappenhaus where the tour forms up. It is not strenuous and it is not optional.

The one thing genuinely on top of the three hours is parking, because it happens before you reach the valley station. Salzwelten states plainly that it has no visitor car park of its own and advises allowing at least an hour to find a space in the village. That is the operator's figure, not ours, and in August it is not pessimistic.

€49 or €33 — and the path that is shut

The €16 difference between the two tickets is the funicular, and the cheaper fare exists for a specific person: someone who walks up the salt mountain instead of riding. This season that person cannot do what the ticket is for.

The direct route up, the Serpentinenweg, is closed for construction until spring 2027. The alternative, round through the Echerntal, takes ninety minutes to two hours one way — and then you have the fifteen-minute approach and the ninety-minute tour, and you still have to get down. Salzwelten sells the €33 ticket without mentioning any of this; the closure is published by the Hallstatt tourist office, and the two organisations do not cross-reference each other. If you buy the hiker's fare this autumn to save €16, it currently costs you half a day.

Worth knowing before you flinch at €49: Salzwelten runs three mines, and this is the expensive one. Altaussee is €27. Salzburg, at Bad Dürrnberg, is €35 and leans Celtic rather than prehistoric. There is no combined ticket across the three — we checked all three price pages. Since the Hallstatt tour alone at €33 sits neatly between the other two, the premium you are paying is essentially the cable car.

What is actually unique here

A staircase someone climbed in 1343 BC

Picture a man coming up out of the dark with a load of salt on his back, on wooden stairs wide enough that the man going down can pass him without either of them stopping. That happened here, in this mountain, in the fourteenth century BC. The stairs he used are still standing, four hundred metres inside the rock, and you walk past them on the standard tour.

They outlasted him, the Roman Empire, the Middle Ages and the invention of the thing you are reading this on, because salt does not let wood rot. This is the oldest preserved wooden staircase in Europe and it is the reason to buy the ticket. The slides are fun and the new funicular is very good, but they are not why you come.

Now the evidence, which is unusually good. The timbers were felled in 1344 and 1343 BC and the staircase was built in 1343 — dated to the exact year by tree rings, which only became possible after Austrian researchers spent years assembling a reference curve from spruce and larch grown at 900 to 1600 metres, because wood from that altitude does not match the lowland curves. It is eight metres long, with treads 1.20 metres wide, and it was designed to come apart: the Bronze Age builders made it modular, so a broken part could be swapped out.

How complete is it? The Natural History Museum Vienna, which runs the excavations, says the staircase survived nahezu unversehrt — almost undamaged — and we would keep the "almost". By the time it was excavated, rock pressure had been shifting and lifting the structure for years, which is precisely why it had to be moved. The museum's own documents put the number of individual pieces at more than seventy in one press release and at sixty in another; nobody has reconciled them.

What you are looking at is the original wood, but not quite where it was found. The staircase was fully exposed in 2012 to stop the mountain crushing it, taken apart from the bottom up from 2013, laser-scanned piece by piece, dried under a purpose-built procedure at the University of Natural Resources in Vienna and CT-scanned at the Austrian Foundry Research Institute. A new chamber was blasted out of stable rock over the winter of 2014–15, the staircase was re-erected there in the spring, bedded in finely ground rock salt, and the Bronze Age Cinema opened on 25 April 2015. It now sits behind a diffusion-tight transparent film with six sensors monitoring the climate around it, and a 4K animation of Bronze Age working life projected onto the find itself. No part of it was replaced with new timber.

Two cautions if you go reading further. Some visitor pages still say the staircase is in situ, stuck where it was found; it has not been for over a decade. And the Natural History Museum carries 1108 BC on one of its own English pages, a figure contradicted by its own press material, its own wood-analysis page and the dendrochronology lab that did the work, all of which give 1344 and 1343. Competitors copy the wrong one.

What the miners ate

They had blue cheese and beer. Three thousand years ago, underground, in the Austrian Alps.

We know because of the other thing salt preserves. Researchers analysed human palaeofaeces recovered from the mine, spanning the Bronze Age to the eighteenth century, and published the results in Current Biology in 2021. One Iron Age sample carried Penicillium roqueforti and Saccharomyces cerevisiae in quantities and genetic configurations that indicate deliberate fermentation — the first molecular evidence of blue cheese and beer in Iron Age Europe. The rest of the diet was heavy on cereal bran and broad beans, and the miners' gut flora resembled that of modern non-Westernised populations right through to the Baroque.

That is the Hallstatt effect in one finding. Salt is hygroscopic and antimicrobial, and the collapsed Bronze Age galleries sealed everything inside them, which is why this site yields wood, leather, textile, rope and dinner where other sites yield only metal and pottery.

How old is it, really — and the blind spot the place created

You will see "7,000 years" everywhere, including on our own pages, and it needs a qualification. Seven thousand years refers to salt production here, and it rests on an antler pick found in 1838 and radiocarbon-dated to roughly 5000 BC. The Natural History Museum, which holds it, is careful: the pick "might indicate underground mining" but "might equally have been used to open up a salt spring".

Underground mining is a shorter and better-evidenced story, and the sources still disagree. The museum dates the oldest direct evidence to the fifteenth century BC and says that is probably not the beginning; the dendrochronologists put the first timbers at 1458 BC; the peer-reviewed synthesis will only commit to the fourteenth century BC. A single start date is not available, and anyone who gives you one is rounding.

The end of that first phase is sharper. The youngest piece of wood anywhere in the Bronze Age mine dates to 1245 BC, and the museum links the collapse of the workings to a debris flow — a mudslide of saturated rock, not a dry rockfall — at about that time. Mining resumes in the ninth century BC, in a different part of the mountain, after a gap of some four hundred years.

And then the place created a problem for the science that named itself after it. Radiocarbon dating has a flat stretch between roughly 750 and 400 BC where the calibration curve cannot separate one date from another. Because it covers exactly the period this cemetery defines, it is known in the literature as the Hallstatt plateau. The village named an archaeological age, and then named the blind spot that makes that age hard to date.

None of this is finished. In September 2026 the museum announced a previously unknown Iron Age complex found by core drilling: parallel chambers stacked above and below one another, running at least 300 metres, each around 25 metres wide and 20 metres high. That is a press announcement rather than a published paper, so treat it as news rather than settled — but it is why the mountain is still interesting to people who are not tourists.

A correction we owe the village museum, because this is the point most guides get backwards. The World Heritage Museum is not a room of replicas and screens: the regional tourist office describes it as holding "numerous original finds from the Hallstatt burial grounds", arranged across twenty-six themed sections running from the Neolithic to the arrival of tourism, with the multimedia staging on top of the objects rather than instead of them. What is true is that the biggest and best-known part of the material excavated by Johann Georg Ramsauer in the nineteenth century is in Vienna, four hours away. Both things are true at once, and a competitor who tells you the village museum has nothing real in it has not been in.

One more reason to put it on the list this year: a special exhibition on the recent Riezinger excavations runs there from April 2026 until December 2027.

As for the gold — fourteen pieces, 506 grams, found on the Arikogel above the lake — the original has been in the Natural History Museum's gold cabinet in Vienna since 2015. It did come home once: the museum lent the originals back to the village for a single summer in 2015, while its own prehistory halls were being refitted. We could find no published statement either way about whether a copy is on display in Hallstatt now, so do not go looking for it on our word.

Seventeen stops from the ferry pier to the World Heritage viewpoint, about ninety minutes, map and audio offline. It does not go into the mine — that tour has its own guide — but it is what turns the hours either side of it into something more than waiting.

Planning the day, and not missing the last tour

The closing time is not the arrival time. "9:30–16:30" means the last tour starts at 16:30, and the latest funicular ascent is half an hour before that. From 2 November the last tour starts at 15:00. In November, aiming for "mid-afternoon" misses the mine entirely.

Do not plan around the last slot without confirming it. The operator and the village tourist site publish different winter timetables: Salzwelten gives a season from 2 November and a last funicular descent at 16:30, the tourist site gives 25 October and 16:00. We would not rely on either for the final tour of the day — book the slot, or ring the operator, rather than turning up for it and having to negotiate with the staff.

There is no visitor parking. Salzwelten has none of its own and says to allow at least an hour. P2 is a minute from the valley station, but the municipality lists it as summer-only, so from November the nearest reliable car park is P1 at about seven minutes' walk. P3 and P4 are around thirty.

Closed on 24 and 31 December — two dates a Christmas visitor is unusually likely to pick. The market itself is one day, 8 December, and the mine is open that day.

The winter shape of the village is the opposite of what most blogs say. The mine and the funicular run on short hours until 6 January and then stop, with nothing published for 2027 at all. What stays open through the rest of the winter is in the village: the World Heritage Museum daily, the charnel house Wednesday to Sunday from November to March at €2, and the free archaeological excavation under the sports shop on Seestrasse, open all year. So a January visit is not an empty one — it is just not a mine visit.

Two day shapes work. Arrive early, do the mine first and have the village in the afternoon and evening, when the day-trip coaches have gone. Or walk the village in the quiet of the morning and take an afternoon tour, remembering the 15:00 last tour after 2 November. Which month you come changes all of this more than anything else, and our month-by-month guide has the full calendar; our complete guide to Hallstatt frames the rest of the day, and for getting here at all there is Salzburg and Vienna.

What the 2026 rebuild changed

Salzwelten closed the funicular in September 2025 and reopened on 29 August 2026. The operator's own press release puts the works at 362 days and the investment at about €34 million — worth saying where that figure comes from, since it circulates without a source: it is in Salzwelten's own announcement, not a journalist's estimate.

Three things changed that a visitor actually notices. The old line was dismantled and an entirely new one built on the same alignment, so nothing you read before autumn 2026 describes the ride you will get. The climb now takes under three minutes in glass cabins that hold themselves level on the slope. And boarding is step-free, with a bistro and terrace at the top and a large shop at the bottom.

What did not change matters as much. The tour underground is the same walk through the same mountain, and the mine still cannot be visited in a wheelchair.

What €34 million built

593 metres of track, 325 metres of height gain between the valley station at 515 m and the mountain station at 840 m. Maximum gradient 87.72 per cent. 5.5 metres per second, journey time 2.6 minutes, two cabins of 60 people, 1,000 people an hour. Cable-car technology by Garaventa, cabins by Carvatech near Gmunden, architecture by Hasenauer.Architekten. At the top, a bistro called Weitblick 7000; at the bottom, a 300 m² shop.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the Hallstatt salt mine cost?
€49 for an adult and €23 for a child aged 4 to 15, including the funicular up and down. The mine tour alone, for people walking up, is €33 and €16; the funicular alone is €29 and €14. Groups of twenty or more pay €47. There is no family ticket and no online discount — the operator states that concessions are given only in person at the ticket window.

How long does the Hallstatt salt mine take?
The guided tour underground is about ninety minutes and covers roughly two kilometres. For the whole visit the operator's own pages say either two and a half hours or three, depending which one you read — and both figures are measured from the valley station and back, already including the funicular and the fifteen-minute walk to the entrance, so there is nothing to add on top. Allow three hours, plus up to an hour for parking before that if you drive.

Is the Hallstatt salt mine worth it?
If you have three clear hours, we think so: it holds the oldest preserved wooden staircase in Europe, dated to 1343 BC, and Salzwelten calls it the oldest salt mine in the world. On a four-hour day trip it will consume the visit. If the Dachstein cable cars are also on your list, treat them as two different days.

Is the Hallstatt salt mine really the oldest in the world?
That is the operator's claim, and it is a reasonable one for salt mining in Europe — but it is a claim, not a settled fact. Archaeologists working at Duzdagi in Azerbaijan date rock-salt extraction there to the second half of the fifth millennium BC, older than anything documented at Hallstatt. Note too that Salzwelten gives a different superlative to its own Hallein mine, which it calls the oldest show mine in the world because visitors have been going down it since 1607. The hard, checkable record Hallstatt holds is the staircase.

How cold is it inside the Hallstatt salt mine?
About 8 °C all year, on the operator's figure. Bring a layer and wear proper shoes; the ground is uneven in places. Protective overalls are compulsory and are provided.

Do you have to go down the slides?
No. Stairs with a handrail run alongside the miners' slides, including the 64-metre one, and you can take them instead.

Can you visit the Hallstatt salt mine in a wheelchair?
No. The new funicular and the bistro are step-free, but the operator states that conditions inside the mine make a wheelchair visit impossible. Children are admitted from age 4, a limit set by the Austrian mining act.

When is the Hallstatt salt mine open in winter?
From 2 November 2026 to 6 January 2027 the last tour starts at 15:00, and the mine is closed on 24 and 31 December. Nothing is published beyond 6 January 2027 — as of 22 October 2026 there is no 2027 spring date anywhere, so a visit after the New Year has to be confirmed with the operator.

The verdict

Worth three hours, mostly for an eight-metre wooden staircase that someone built in 1343 BC and that is still standing because salt does not let things rot. The tour itself is underground and runs whatever the sky is doing; the funicular and the quarter-hour walk across an open high valley are not, so weather still shapes the day even when it does not cancel it. In cloud the mine shows you what it always shows you, and the village does not — which is an argument about the order you do things in, not a promise that the mountain is always open.

Not worth it on a short day trip, not worth it on the same day as Dachstein, and — this season specifically — not worth saving €16 on, because the path that cheaper ticket is designed for is shut until spring.
